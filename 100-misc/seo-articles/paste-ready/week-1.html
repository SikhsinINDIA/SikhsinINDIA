7 Simple Ways to Teach Children About Sikhi at Home

Practical, screen-light ideas for parents to teach kids about the Sikh Gurus, Gurbani and values, using sakhis, quizzes and everyday seva.

Many Sikh parents, in India and abroad, tell the same story: the children love going to the gurudwara for langar, but they don't really know who the Gurus were or why we do what we do. Sunday school helps, but children learn most from what happens at home.

The good news is that it doesn't take a lot of time. Here are seven ideas that have worked for families we know.

1. Tell one sakhi at bedtime

Sakhis, short stories from the lives of the Gurus, are made for children. Each has a clear moral and a memorable scene: Guru Nanak Dev Ji spending his trading money feeding hungry sadhus (Sacha Sauda), or counting "tera, tera" (yours, yours) as he weighed grain. One story a night, told in your own words, does more than an hour-long lecture.

If you need a starting point, here's a collection of short sakhis written for children, including Bhai Lalo and Malik Bhago, Sajjan Thug and Vali Kandhari.

2. Ask "What would you have done?"

After the story, ask a question. Why did Guru Ji choose Bhai Lalo's simple roti over Malik Bhago's feast? Children remember what they've had to think about.

3. Learn the ten Gurus in order, as a game

Write the ten Gurus' names on cards and race to put them in order. Then add one fact per Guru. Within a few weeks most children can name all ten and tell you something about each.

4. Make quizzes a family competition

Children love to beat their parents. Try these free Sikh history quizzes for kids on the Gurus, the Panj Pyare and the Panj Takhts, and keep a scoreboard on the fridge.

5. Learn five Gurmukhi letters a week

Ura, Aira, Iri, Sassa, Haha. Five letters a week means the whole painti (35 letters) in about two months. Point them out on gurudwara signboards and in the Gutka Sahib.

6. Let them do real seva

Take children to help in langar: folding napkins, handing out water, sweeping. Seva isn't explained; it's experienced. Afterwards, ask how it felt.

7. Say Ardaas together, briefly

End the day with a short Ardaas in your own words, thanking Waheguru and remembering someone who needs help. Children pick up rhythm and gratitude long before they understand every word.

A final thought

You don't have to be an expert to pass on Sikhi. You just have to be consistent. One story, one question and one small act of seva a day adds up to a childhood rooted in the Gurus' teachings.

What has worked in your family? Share your ideas in the comments.

Ten Gurudwaras in Delhi Every Visitor Should Know, and the History Behind Each

Most visitors to Delhi see the Red Fort, Qutub Minar and India Gate. Few realise that a short walk from some of those monuments stand gurudwaras that mark turning points in Sikh history: a Guru's martyrdom, an epidemic met with service, a meeting between a Guru and an emperor.

Every gurudwara is open to everyone, whatever their faith, and every one serves free food. If you have a free afternoon in Delhi, here are ten worth knowing.

1. Gurudwara Bangla Sahib, Connaught Place

The most visited gurudwara in the city was once a bungalow belonging to Raja Jai Singh. The eighth Guru, Guru Har Krishan Ji, stayed here in 1664 as a child, when smallpox and cholera were sweeping Delhi. He tended the sick, and the well water he gave out is remembered in the sarovar (holy pool) that visitors still sit beside today. The langar hall here feeds thousands of people a day.

2. Gurudwara Sis Ganj Sahib, Chandni Chowk

In the middle of Old Delhi's busiest bazaar is the place where Guru Tegh Bahadur Ji, the ninth Guru, was executed in 1675 for refusing to give up his faith, and for defending the right of others to practise theirs. Sikhs call him Hind di Chadar, the Shield of India.

3. Gurudwara Rakab Ganj Sahib, near Parliament House

After the Guru's martyrdom, Lakhi Shah Vanjara carried his body away and cremated it by setting fire to his own house so the Mughal authorities wouldn't notice. Rakab Ganj Sahib stands on that spot, today just steps from India's Parliament.

4. Gurudwara Bala Sahib

Where Guru Har Krishan Ji was cremated in 1664 at the age of eight. Memorials to Mata Sundari Ji and Mata Sahib Kaur Ji are also here.

5. Gurudwara Mata Sundari Ji

Near ITO, this marks where Mata Sundari Ji, wife of Guru Gobind Singh Ji, lived and guided the Sikh community for decades after the Guru's passing.

6. Gurudwara Majnu Ka Tilla Sahib

On the banks of the Yamuna in north Delhi, this commemorates Guru Nanak Dev Ji's meeting with a Sufi mystic known locally as "Majnu". It is one of the oldest Sikh sites in the city.

7. Gurudwara Nanak Piao Sahib

Guru Nanak Dev Ji is said to have stayed here on his travels and given water (piao) to thirsty travellers, a tradition of service the gurudwara continues.

8. Gurudwara Damdama Sahib

Near Humayun's Tomb, linked to Guru Gobind Singh Ji's time in Delhi in 1707 and his meeting with the Mughal prince Bahadur Shah.

9. Gurudwara Moti Bagh Sahib

Associated with Guru Gobind Singh Ji's stay in Delhi. The story goes that the Guru announced his arrival by sending an arrow to the Red Fort.

10. Shahidi Asthan Baba Banda Singh Bahadur, Mehrauli

Where Baba Banda Singh Bahadur and his companions were martyred in 1716 after refusing to renounce Sikhi.

You'll find photos, addresses and fuller histories for each of these in this guide to the historic gurudwaras of Delhi.

Visiting etiquette, in one minute

  • Cover your head. Bring a scarf; most gurudwaras also lend head coverings at the entrance.
  • Remove your shoes at the shoe counter (it's free, run by volunteers) and wash your hands and feet.
  • No tobacco or alcohol on the premises.
  • Sit on the floor in the prayer hall and in the langar hall. Everyone eats together, sitting side by side as equals. That's the point.
  • Accept the karah prashad (sweet offering) with both hands cupped.
  • Photography: usually fine in the courtyard, but be respectful inside the main hall.

Why these places matter

Many of Delhi's gurudwaras commemorate sacrifice made for someone else's freedom: Guru Tegh Bahadur Ji gave his life defending the right of Kashmiri Pandits to keep their faith. When you sit in the langar hall beside strangers from every background, you're taking part in that same idea.

If Delhi leaves you curious, the same site keeps a state-by-state gurudwara directory covering the rest of India, from Patna Sahib in Bihar to Hazur Sahib in Maharashtra.
