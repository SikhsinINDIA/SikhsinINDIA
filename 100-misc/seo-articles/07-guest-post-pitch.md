## Article

### The Seva Nobody Sees: Sikh Welfare Organisations Quietly Saving Lives Across India

Most people know about langar, the free meal served at every gurudwara. Fewer know that the same idea, *seva*, selfless service with no expectation of reward, has produced a network of welfare organisations across India that run blood banks, ambulance services, free clinics and homes for people nobody else will take in.

They rarely advertise. Here's a look at what they do, and why.

#### Seva is not charity

In Sikh thought, giving isn't a favour from the fortunate to the unfortunate. The Gurus taught that the same Divine light lives in every person, so serving someone else is a form of worship. Sikhs are also encouraged to give *dasvandh*, a tenth of their earnings, to the common good.

That shapes how these organisations work. Volunteers usually come from ordinary jobs. Funding is mostly small, regular donations. And the people served are treated as guests, not cases.

#### Blood donation: a lifeline in Amritsar

Blood shortages are a constant problem in Indian hospitals, especially for thalassemia patients who need transfusions every few weeks. In Amritsar, the **Shri Guru Ram Dass Blood Donation Sewa Society** organises regular camps and maintains a donor network so that families aren't left searching for donors in an emergency.

#### Free medicines and medical aids

Far from Punjab, the **Guru Nanak Medical Sewa Foundation** in Kanpur provides free medicines to people who can't afford them, a reminder that Sikh seva isn't limited to Sikh-majority regions. In Faridabad, the **Jagjit Welfare Trust** lends medical aids to patients at zero rent; families use them as long as they need and return them for the next person.

#### Homes for the helpless

Some of the most demanding seva is long-term care. **Prabh Aasra** in SAS Nagar and the **Manukhta Di Sewa Society** in Ludhiana care for people with severe mental or physical illness who have been abandoned, often found on roadsides with no one to look after them. Volunteers and staff feed, bathe and treat residents around the clock. In Mohali, **Gur Aasra Trust** supports children, orphans, widows and elderly people left without family support.

#### "Tera Hi Tera" and "Sarbat da Bhala"

Many of these groups take their names from Sikh teachings. *Tera hi tera* ("everything is Yours") recalls Guru Nanak Dev Ji counting grain as a storekeeper. When he reached thirteen, *terah*, he kept repeating *tera*, giving away everything as belonging to God. The **Tera Hi Tera Mission** in Chandigarh, a sewa project of Guru Ka Langer Eye Hospital, runs an affordable multi-speciality OPD with diagnostics. *Sarbat da bhala*, "the welfare of all", ends every Sikh Ardaas; the **Sarbat Da Bhala Charitable Trust** in Patiala works on the social and economic development of rural and remote communities.

#### How you can help

- **Donate blood** at a camp near you. Many Sikh societies list camps on social media.
- **Give regularly**, even small amounts. A predictable ₹500 a month often helps more than a one-off large gift.
- **Volunteer time:** cooking, driving, admin, or simply visiting residents.
- **Spread the word.** These organisations do the work; they rarely have time for marketing.

There's a growing [directory of Sikh welfare organisations](https://sikhsinindia.com/05-sikh-welfare/05-sikh-welfare.html) if you'd like to find one near you.

The Gurus measured devotion by how we treat each other. These volunteers take that literally, every day, without an audience.
