## Q1. "What is the meaning of Ik Onkar?"

**Link:** anchor "a fuller explanation of Ik Onkar" → https://sikhsinindia.com/02-sikh-heritage/02-01-discover-sikhi/02-01-05-discover-sikhi/02-01-05-meaning-of-ik-onkar.html

> **ੴ (Ik Onkar)** is the first phrase of the Guru Granth Sahib and the opening of the Mool Mantar. It's made of two parts:
>
> - **੧ (Ik)**: the numeral *one*. It's written as a number, not a word, to leave no ambiguity: there is exactly one Creator.
> - **ਓਅੰਕਾਰ (Onkar)**: the Creator who is present throughout creation. The open curve extending from the letter is often read as that presence being limitless.
>
> So Ik Onkar is usually translated as **"There is One Creator, pervading all."** It's both a statement about God and about people: if the same One is in everyone, then caste, gender and religion can't make anyone higher or lower than anyone else. That idea runs through Sikh practice, from sitting together in langar to the Gurus' defence of other faiths.
>
> It's also why you'll see ੴ at the top of almost every Sikh text and on gurudwara walls: it sets the context for everything that follows.
>
> If you're interested, here's [a fuller explanation of Ik Onkar](https://sikhsinindia.com/02-sikh-heritage/02-01-discover-sikhi/02-01-05-discover-sikhi/02-01-05-meaning-of-ik-onkar.html) with the rest of the Mool Mantar.

## Q2. "What are the 5 Ks in Sikhism and what do they mean?"

**Link:** anchor "The Panj Kakaar" → https://sikhsinindia.com/02-sikh-heritage/02-01-discover-sikhi/02-01-02-5ks.html

> The 5 Ks (*Panj Kakaar*) are five articles of faith that initiated (Amritdhari) Sikhs keep at all times, as instructed by Guru Gobind Singh Ji at the creation of the Khalsa in 1699. Each begins with the letter "K" in Punjabi:
>
> 1. **Kesh**: uncut hair, accepting the body as the Creator made it. Usually covered with a turban (dastar) or chunni.
> 2. **Kangha**: a small wooden comb, for keeping the hair clean. It's a reminder of discipline and cleanliness of mind as well as body.
> 3. **Kara**: a steel bracelet. A circle has no beginning or end, like God; steel stands for strength. It also reminds the wearer that their hands should do nothing the Guru wouldn't approve of.
> 4. **Kachera**: cotton undershorts, a symbol of self-restraint and readiness for action.
> 5. **Kirpan**: a small sword, a commitment to defend the weak and stand against injustice. It's a symbol of duty, not aggression.
>
> Together they form a uniform: a Sikh is visibly identifiable, which means visibly accountable for how they behave.
>
> More detail on each: [The Panj Kakaar](https://sikhsinindia.com/02-sikh-heritage/02-01-discover-sikhi/02-01-02-5ks.html). *(I help run that site.)*

## Q3. "Can non-Sikhs visit a gurudwara? What should I know before going?"

**Link:** anchor "gurudwaras by state" → https://sikhsinindia.com/04-gurudwaras/04-gurudwaras-in-india.html

> Yes, absolutely. Every gurudwara is open to everyone regardless of religion, caste, gender or nationality. Gurudwaras traditionally have four doors to symbolise that welcome. Here's what to know:
>
> - **Cover your head.** A scarf, bandana or handkerchief is fine. Most gurudwaras have spare head coverings at the entrance.
> - **Shoes off.** Leave them at the shoe counter (free, run by volunteers) and wash your hands and feet if there's a footbath.
> - **No tobacco, alcohol or intoxicants** anywhere on the premises.
> - **Inside the main hall**, people bow to the Guru Granth Sahib. You're welcome to, but not required to. Just sit quietly on the floor, and don't point your feet towards the Guru Granth Sahib.
> - **Karah prashad**: you'll be offered a sweet, buttery offering. Accept it with both hands cupped.
> - **Langar**: you'll be invited to eat a free vegetarian meal, sitting on the floor in rows with everyone else. Please do. It's the heart of the experience.
> - **Photos**: fine in courtyards, but ask or be discreet inside.
>
> Nobody will try to convert you. You'll just be treated as a guest. If you're travelling in India, there's a directory of [gurudwaras by state](https://sikhsinindia.com/04-gurudwaras/04-gurudwaras-in-india.html) with histories and locations. *(I help run that site.)*

## Q4. "What is Nitnem in Sikhism?"

**Link:** anchor "Nitnem banis" → https://sikhsinindia.com/03-nitnem/03-nitnem-path.html

> *Nitnem* (ਨਿਤਨੇਮ) means "daily routine". It's the set of prayers (banis) Sikhs recite every day.
>
> **Morning (Amrit vela, before dawn):** Japji Sahib, Jaap Sahib, Tav-Prasad Savaiye, Chaupai Sahib and Anand Sahib.
> **Evening:** Rehras Sahib.
> **Night, before sleeping:** Kirtan Sohila.
>
> Most people take 45–60 minutes in the morning, less once they know the banis well. The point isn't ritual for its own sake: it's to begin and end each day by remembering Waheguru (*Naam Simran*), so the rest of the day's work is done in that frame of mind.
>
> If you're starting out, begin with Japji Sahib alone and add one bani at a time. You can read or listen to the [Nitnem banis](https://sikhsinindia.com/03-nitnem/03-nitnem-path.html) online while you learn. *(I help run that site.)*

## Q5 (no link: credibility builder). "Why do Sikhs serve free food (langar)?"

> Langar goes back to Guru Nanak Dev Ji in the early 1500s and was made a central institution by Guru Amar Das Ji. It does three things at once:
>
> 1. **Equality:** everyone sits on the floor and eats the same food. In a society organised by caste, where people of different castes wouldn't eat together, this was radical.
> 2. **Seva:** it's run by volunteers. Cooking, serving and washing up are forms of worship.
> 3. **Feeding the hungry:** anyone can walk in, no questions asked.
>
> The tradition continues today at every gurudwara, from small village ones to Sri Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar, which feeds tens of thousands of people a day.
