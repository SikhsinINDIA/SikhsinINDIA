Many Sikh parents, in India and abroad, tell the same story: the children love going to the gurudwara for langar, but they don't really know who the Gurus were or why we do what we do. Sunday school helps, but children learn most from what happens at home.

The good news is that it doesn't take a lot of time. Here are seven ideas that have worked for families we know.

## 1. Tell one sakhi at bedtime

Sakhis, short stories from the lives of the Gurus, are made for children. Each has a clear moral and a memorable scene: Guru Nanak Dev Ji spending his trading money feeding hungry sadhus (*Sacha Sauda*), or counting "tera, tera" (*yours, yours*) as he weighed grain. One story a night, told in your own words, does more than an hour-long lecture.

If you need a starting point, here's a collection of [short sakhis written for children](https://sikhsinindia.com/07-sikh-section-for-kids/07-01-sikh-stories/07-sikh-stories.html), including Bhai Lalo and Malik Bhago, Sajjan Thug and Vali Kandhari.

## 2. Ask "What would you have done?"

After the story, ask a question. *Why did Guru Ji choose Bhai Lalo's simple roti over Malik Bhago's feast?* Children remember what they've had to think about.

## 3. Learn the ten Gurus in order, as a game

Write the ten Gurus' names on cards and race to put them in order. Then add one fact per Guru. Within a few weeks most children can name all ten and tell you something about each.

## 4. Make quizzes a family competition

Children love to beat their parents. Try these [free Sikh history quizzes for kids](https://sikhsinindia.com/07-sikh-section-for-kids/07-02-quiz-for-kids/07-02-quiz-for-kids.html) on the Gurus, the Panj Pyare and the Panj Takhts, and keep a scoreboard on the fridge.

## 5. Learn five Gurmukhi letters a week

Ura, Aira, Iri, Sassa, Haha. Five letters a week means the whole *painti* (35 letters) in about two months. Point them out on gurudwara signboards and in the Gutka Sahib.

## 6. Let them do real seva

Take children to help in langar: folding napkins, handing out water, sweeping. Seva isn't explained; it's experienced. Afterwards, ask how it felt.

## 7. Say Ardaas together, briefly

End the day with a short Ardaas in your own words, thanking Waheguru and remembering someone who needs help. Children pick up rhythm and gratitude long before they understand every word.

## A final thought

You don't have to be an expert to pass on Sikhi. You just have to be consistent. One story, one question and one small act of seva a day adds up to a childhood rooted in the Gurus' teachings.
