Every day, in thousands of gurudwaras around the world, volunteers cook and serve a free meal to anyone who walks in. Nobody is asked their religion, caste, income or reason for being there. At Sri Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar, the kitchen feeds tens of thousands of people a day, almost entirely through volunteers.

This is *langar*, and it has run continuously for about five hundred years. I've spent a lot of time around langar kitchens, and I think it's one of the best leadership case studies I know. Here are five lessons.

## 1. Remove the hierarchy from the room, literally

Guru Nanak Dev Ji started the tradition of a shared meal, and Guru Amar Das Ji made it a rule: *pehle pangat, piche sangat*, "first sit in the row, then join the congregation." The story goes that even the Mughal emperor Akbar sat on the floor and ate langar alongside everyone else before meeting the Guru.

Everyone sits at the same level and eats the same food. No VIP table.

**For leaders:** the signals you send through seating, access and perks often speak louder than your values statement.

## 2. Service is the training programme

In a langar kitchen nobody hands you a job description. You start by washing dishes, then peel onions, then roll rotis, and one day you're running the dal. People learn by serving next to people who've done it longer.

**For leaders:** apprenticeship beats onboarding slides. Put new people next to experienced ones on real work.

## 3. Scale through a simple, repeatable system

Feeding fifty thousand people a day without a central command centre sounds impossible. It works because the menu is simple, the roles are clear, and the process is the same every day. Anyone can step into a station and know what to do.

**For leaders:** before you scale, make the work simple enough that a newcomer can contribute in ten minutes.

## 4. Dignity is part of the product

Langar isn't charity handed down; it's a meal shared across. The person serving you may eat beside you an hour later. The respect is part of what's on the plate.

**For leaders:** how you deliver a service (to customers, employees or communities) matters as much as what you deliver.

## 5. Contribution is a habit, not an event

Sikhs are taught *dasvandh*, giving a tenth of one's earnings, and *seva*, selfless service. Langar keeps going because thousands of people contribute a little, consistently, rather than a few contributing a lot, once.

**For leaders:** cultures are built by small, repeated behaviours, not annual initiatives.
