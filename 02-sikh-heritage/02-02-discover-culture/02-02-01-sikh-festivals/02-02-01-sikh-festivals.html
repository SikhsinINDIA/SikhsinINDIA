Sikh Festivals

Sikh New Year

Chet Sangrand marks the first day of the Nanakshahi calendar, welcoming the Sikh New Year with prayers and gratitude. It falls on the first of the month of Chet, usually in mid-March. Sangat gather at Gurdwaras for Ardas and kirtan to seek blessings for the year ahead. The day symbolizes renewal, hope, and a fresh beginning rooted in faith.

Lohri

Lohri is a vibrant harvest festival celebrated with bonfires, folk songs, and sharing of til, gajak, and popcorn. For Sikhs, it carries deep cultural meaning tied to community, gratitude, and standing up for justice. It's often associated with the legend of Dulla Bhatti, who fought for the poor and oppressed. Families and neighbours gather around the fire, singing traditional Punjabi songs and exchanging good wishes.

Holla Mohalla

Holla Mohalla was instituted by Guru Gobind Singh Ji at Anandpur Sahib as a display of Sikh martial spirit. Held the day after Holi, it features mock battles, horse-riding, and displays of swordsmanship by the Nihang Singhs. The festival reflects the Khalsa's readiness to defend righteousness and protect the weak. It remains one of the most spectacular gatherings in Sikh tradition, drawing thousands to Anandpur Sahib.

Holla Mohalla - The Festival of Martial Spirit

Baisakhi

Baisakhi commemorates the founding of the Khalsa by Guru Gobind Singh Ji in 1699 at Anandpur Sahib. On this day, the Guru baptized the Panj Pyare and gave Sikhs a distinct identity through the Amrit ceremony. It is also celebrated as a harvest festival across Punjab, marking the ripening of the Rabi crop. Gurdwaras hold special Nagar Kirtans, and the day is observed with deep reverence and joyous celebration alike.

Maghi

Maghi is one of three festivals — alongside Vaisakhi and Diwali — chosen by Guru Amar Das Ji for Sikhs to gather and celebrate together. It commemorates the sacrifice of the Chali Mukte (Forty Liberated Ones), who laid down their lives defending Guru Gobind Singh Ji at the Battle of Muktsar. A grand mela is held annually at Muktsar to honour their courage and devotion. The festival is a reminder of unwavering loyalty and sacrifice in the face of adversity.

Bandi Chhor Divas

Bandi Chhor Divas celebrates the release of Guru Hargobind Ji from Gwalior Fort, along with 52 princes he refused to leave behind. The Guru insisted the emperor free the kings too, tying ribbons to his cloak so each could hold on and walk free. The day coincides with Diwali and is marked with the lighting of diyas at Gurdwaras, especially the Golden Temple. It stands as a powerful symbol of compassion, justice, and freedom for all.

Mother's Day

Mother's Day honours the boundless love, strength, and sacrifice of mothers everywhere. In Sikh tradition, the mother's role is deeply revered, echoing teachings on nurturing and selfless devotion found in Gurbani. It's a day to express gratitude for her guidance and unconditional support. Families come together to celebrate the quiet grace and resilience mothers bring to every home.

Mother's Day - Honouring Maternal Love

Father's Day

Father's Day celebrates the guidance, protection, and quiet strength fathers provide throughout life. Sikh teachings emphasize the father's role in shaping character, courage, and values within the family. It's an occasion to honour his sacrifices and the wisdom passed down through generations. Families mark the day with gratitude, recognizing the steady presence fathers bring to their lives.
