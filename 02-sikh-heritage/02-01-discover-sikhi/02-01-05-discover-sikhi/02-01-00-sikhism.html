Brief Answer

Who Are the Sikhs?

Sikhs are followers of Sikhism, a distinct monotheistic [belief in one, formless, eternal God (Ik Onkar)] religion founded in 1469 CE by Guru Nanak Dev Ji in the Punjab region. Arising in a 15th-century South Asia dominated by Hinduism and Islam, Sikhism emerged not as a blend of existing faiths, but as an independent spiritual revelation. It rejected prevalent empty ritualism, priestly monopolies, social oppression, and the caste system in favor of belief in One God (Ik Onkar), absolute human and gender equality, honest living, and selfless service (Sewa).

Descriptive Answer

The World’s Fifth-Largest Organised Religion

Sikhs are adherents of Sikhism, the world’s fifth-largest organised religion, founded by Guru Nanak Dev Ji in 1469 CE and guided by nine successor Gurus and the eternal scripture, Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji.

Why a New Religion Emerged

During the 15th century, South Asian society was deeply fragmented by ritualism, priestcraft, religious persecution, and severe caste and gender discrimination under established Hindu and Islamic structures. Following a divine revelation (“Na koi hindu, na koi musalman” — “There is no Hindu, there is no Musalman”), Guru Nanak Dev Ji revealed a distinct path based on direct communion with God, social justice, and universal human brotherhood.

Core Principles & Practices

  • Ik Onkar: Belief in one supreme, formless God residing within all creation.
  • Universal Equality: Complete rejection of the caste system, religious elitism, and gender inequality — granting women equal spiritual and social status.

Three Daily Pillars:

Naam Japna
Remembering God through meditation and prayer
Kirat Karo
Earning an honest, householder living without ascetic renunciation
Vand Chhako
Sharing earnings through charity and free community kitchens (Langar)

The Khalsa & Five Ks: Initiated Sikhs maintain physical articles of faith — including uncut hair (Kesh) covered by a turban — symbolising spiritual discipline, moral purity, and a commitment to protect the vulnerable against oppression (Miri-Piri).

Elaborate/Comprehensive Answer

An Independent Monotheistic Tradition

Sikhs (meaning “learners” or “disciples”) belong to an independent monotheistic [belief in one, formless, eternal God (Ik Onkar)] tradition founded in 1469 CE by Guru Nanak Dev Ji in the Punjab region. Spiritual authority was developed through a lineage of Ten Human Gurus from 1469 to 1708, after which it was permanently bestowed upon the scripture, Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji, and temporal guidance upon the community body, the Guru Khalsa Panth.

1. Socio-Historical Cause for a New Religion

In 15th-century India, society was spiritually and socially stagnant under dominant Hindu and Islamic systems:

  • Rejection of Empty Ritualism & Clerical Control: Religion had become dominated by superstitious rituals, priestly monopolies, and language barriers. Guru Nanak Dev Ji taught that salvation is reached directly through devotion (Naam Simran) and truthful conduct, requiring no intermediaries.
  • Eradication of the Caste System: Society was divided by rigid birth-based hierarchies. Sikhism established the institution of Langar (free community kitchen), forcing priests, kings, and outcasts to sit side-by-side on the floor as equals. Later, the tenth master gave all men the surname Singh (Lion) and all women Kaur (Princess) to erase caste-identifying names.
  • Restoration of Women’s Dignity: Practices like widow-burning (Sati), female infanticide, and treating women as spiritually inferior were widespread. Sikhism condemned these practices, affording women complete spiritual, social, and leadership equality.
  • Householder Life vs. Asceticism: While many traditions advocated abandoning families to live in forests as yogis, Sikhism rejected ascetic renunciation. It mandated living actively in the world (Grasthi Jeevan), earning an honest living, and maintaining a family.
  • Defense Against Tyranny: In response to religious persecution and forced conversions, Sikhism established the concept of Miri-Piri (balancing spiritual power and temporal sovereignty). When peaceful appeals failed, the Gurus organised the community to resist oppression, culminating in the creation of the Khalsa saint-soldier order.

2. The Declaration of a Distinct Path

After a profound three-day divine experience at the Kali Bein river around 1499 CE, Guru Nanak Dev Ji declared:

“Na koi hindu, na koi musalman”

There is no Hindu, there is no Musalman

This proclaimed that God does not evaluate humanity by religious labels or sectarian dogma, establishing Sikhism as a sovereign, independent faith (Nirmal Panth) focused on moral character and universal brotherhood.

3. The Khalsa Tradition & The Five Ks (Panj Kakar)

In 1699, Guru Gobind Singh Ji formalised the Khalsa — a disciplined body of Sikhs committed to righteousness and defending the innocent. Initiated Sikhs maintain five physical items:

Kesh
Uncut hair, preserving natural form and harmony with divine order (covered by a turban / Dastar)
Kangha
A small wooden comb for cleanliness and orderliness
Kara
An iron bracelet symbolising moral restraint and the eternity of God
Kachera
Designed cotton undergarments symbolising moral purity and readiness
Kirpan
A ceremonial sword representing the duty to protect justice and defend the oppressed

Today, with a global population of 25 to 30 million, Sikhs form a distinct international community renowned for civic engagement, disaster relief, humanitarian service, and an unyielding commitment to human rights.
