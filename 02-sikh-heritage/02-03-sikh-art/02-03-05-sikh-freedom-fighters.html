Sikh Freedom Fighters & Their Sacrifice

🕯 Shaheeds of the Freedom Struggle

Shaheed Mewa Singh (1881–1915)

Martyr for justice in Canada

Mewa Singh was born in 1881 in the village of Lopoke, near Amritsar, and came to British Columbia in 1906 to work in the sawmills. He took Amrit when Vancouver’s first Gurdwara, run by the Khalsa Diwan Society, opened in 1908 and served it devotedly. Sikhs in Canada then faced open racism, with laws and slogans aimed at keeping Canada “white”, and in 1914 the passengers of the Komagata Maru were refused entry. The community, led by Gurdwara president Bhai Bhag Singh, tried to help them; Mewa Singh also worked with the Ghadar movement against British rule in India.

In September 1914 Bela Singh, an informer for immigration inspector William Hopkinson, opened fire inside the Vancouver Gurdwara during a gathering, killing Bhai Bhag Singh and Bhai Battan Singh. Mewa Singh witnessed the killings and refused to give false evidence. On 21 October 1914, at the courthouse where Hopkinson was to testify for Bela Singh, he shot and killed Hopkinson. At his trial he made no denial, saying the shooting in the Gurdwara had compelled him. He was hanged at New Westminster on 11 January 1915, reciting Gurbani, and is remembered every year by Sikhs in Canada as Shaheed Bhai Mewa Singh.

Shaheed Udham Singh (1899–1940)

Avenger of Jallianwala Bagh

Udham Singh was born Sher Singh on 26 December 1899 at Sunam, in the princely state of Patiala. He lost both parents as a child and in 1907 he and his brother were admitted to the Central Khalsa Orphanage in Amritsar, where he received the name Udham Singh. He passed his matriculation in 1918. On 13 April 1919, Baisakhi day, troops under Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer fired on a peaceful crowd of Hindus, Muslims and Sikhs in Jallianwala Bagh, killing hundreds. The massacre, backed by Punjab’s Lieutenant-Governor Sir Michael O’Dwyer, shaped the rest of Udham Singh’s life. He joined the Ghadar movement, admired Bhagat Singh, and was jailed in India from 1927 to 1931 for carrying arms; afterwards he took the name Ram Mohammad Singh Azad, joining the names of India’s three main communities.

On 13 March 1940, at a meeting in Caxton Hall, London, he shot and killed Sir Michael O’Dwyer. He made no attempt to escape and, at his trial, said he had acted against British rule that had left his people starving. He was hanged at Pentonville Prison, London, on 31 July 1940. In 1974 his remains were brought back to India and cremated at Sunam; he is honoured as Shaheed-e-Azam Udham Singh.

Shaheed Bhagat Singh (1907–1931)

Revolutionary and thinker

Bhagat Singh was born on 27 or 28 September 1907 at Banga, in Lyallpur district (now in Pakistan), into a family of freedom fighters. As a young man he joined the Hindustan Republican Association and in 1928 helped reorganise it as the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA). That year Lala Lajpat Rai died after being beaten by police during a protest against the Simon Commission in Lahore. To avenge him, Bhagat Singh and Rajguru shot dead Assistant Superintendent of Police John Saunders on 17 December 1928, mistaking him for the officer responsible.

On 8 April 1929 Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt threw two low-intensity bombs onto empty benches of the Central Legislative Assembly in Delhi, meant to make a point rather than to kill, and courted arrest. In jail he joined fellow prisoner Jatin Das in a hunger strike for the rights of Indian political prisoners; Jatin Das died after 63 days, and Bhagat Singh’s fast lasted 116 days. Convicted in the Saunders case, Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev were hanged in Lahore Central Jail on 23 March 1931. He was 23, and his writings and courage made him one of the best-loved heroes of India’s freedom struggle.
