Sikh Sportsmen & Their Achievements

🏃 Runners

Fauja Singh (1911–2025)

The “Turbaned Tornado”

Fauja Singh was born, by his own account, on 1 April 1911 in Beas Pind near Jalandhar, the youngest of four children in a farming family. After the death of his wife he moved to East London in 1992, and there, in his late eighties, he took up running seriously with coach Harmander Singh. He ran his first London Marathon in 2000, aged 89, and went on to complete nine full marathons. His best time, 5 hours 40 minutes, came at the 2003 Toronto Waterfront Marathon at the age of 92.

In 2004 he appeared alongside David Beckham and Muhammad Ali in Adidas’s Impossible is Nothing campaign. On 16 October 2011, at the Toronto Waterfront Marathon, he became, by his stated age, the first centenarian to finish a marathon, and he retired from competitive running after the Hong Kong 10 km in February 2013. He died on 14 July 2025 after being struck by a vehicle in his home village, aged 114 by his reckoning.

Milkha Singh (1929–2021)

“The Flying Sikh”

Milkha Singh was born on 20 November 1929 (some records give other years) in Govindpura, a village in western Punjab now in Pakistan, and lost much of his family in the violence of Partition. He discovered athletics while serving in the Indian Army. In 1958 he won the 200 m and 400 m at the Asian Games in Tokyo and then the 400 m (440 yards) at the Commonwealth Games in Cardiff, the first Commonwealth gold in athletics for independent India.

At the 1960 Rome Olympics he finished fourth in the 400 m final in a photo finish; his time of 45.73 seconds stood as the Indian record for 38 years. He won two more golds, the 400 m and 4×400 m relay, at the 1962 Asian Games in Jakarta, and competed at three Olympics (1956, 1960 and 1964). He was awarded the Padma Shri, later served as Punjab’s director of sports, and died in Chandigarh on 18 June 2021. His son Jeev Milkha Singh became a professional golfer.

🏏 Cricketers

Bishan Singh Bedi (1946–2023)

Master of left-arm spin

Born on 25 September 1946 in Amritsar, Bishan Singh Bedi was one of the finest slow left-arm bowlers in cricket history and part of India’s famous spin quartet with Erapalli Prasanna, Bhagwat Chandrasekhar and Srinivas Venkataraghavan. Between 1967 and 1979 he played 67 Tests and took 266 wickets, at the time the most by any Indian bowler, and he took 1,560 first-class wickets, more than any other Indian.

He captained India in 22 Tests, winning six, and later managed the Indian team on a tour in 1990. Known for his patka, his elegant action and his frank views on the game, he remained an outspoken voice in Indian cricket until his death in New Delhi on 23 October 2023.

Monty Panesar (b. 1982)

England’s Sikh spinner

Mudhsuden Singh “Monty” Panesar was born on 25 April 1982 in Luton, England, to Sikh parents who had moved from Punjab in 1979. He studied computer science at Loughborough University and played county cricket for Northamptonshire. He made his Test debut for England against India at Nagpur on 1 March 2006, taking his boyhood hero Sachin Tendulkar as his first Test wicket, and became the first Sikh to play Test cricket for England.

His patka and joyful celebrations made him one of the most popular cricketers in Britain. He played in the 2006–07 Ashes series and the 2007 World Cup, and in 50 Tests between 2006 and 2013 he took 167 wickets.

🏑 Hockey Heroes

India’s Olympic Hockey Teams

Sikh players at the heart of Indian hockey

Sikh players have formed the backbone of Indian hockey for generations. Balbir Singh Sr. won three Olympic golds (1948, 1952 and 1956), and at the 1964 Tokyo Olympics India, with stars such as Charanjit Singh, Prithipal Singh, Gurbux Singh and Harbinder Singh, regained the gold by beating Pakistan 1–0 in the final. India also beat Pakistan to win gold at the 1966 Asian Games in Bangkok.

At the 1968 Mexico City Olympics, with Prithipal Singh and Gurbux Singh as joint captains and many Sikh players in the squad, India won the bronze medal, Prithipal Singh again finishing as the tournament’s top scorer. Sikh players such as Ajit Pal Singh, who captained India to the 1975 World Cup title, and, in recent years, Manpreet Singh and Harmanpreet Singh have carried the tradition forward.

🏔 Mountaineers & Scouts

Captain M. S. Kohli (1931–2025)

Leader of India’s first successful Everest expedition

Captain Mohan Singh Kohli was born on 11 December 1931 at Haripur in the North-West Frontier Province (now in Pakistan). An Indian Navy officer, he took part in two earlier Indian attempts on Everest, in 1960 and 1962, and led the first ascents of peaks such as Annapurna III. In 1965 he led the Indian Everest Expedition, which put nine climbers on the summit, a world record that stood for many years.

He later served with the ITBP and Air India, promoted adventure tourism, led India’s first civilian Antarctic expedition, and wrote many books including Nine Atop Everest. His honours include the Padma Bhushan and the Arjuna Award. He died in New Delhi on 23 June 2025, aged 93.

Major H. P. S. Ahluwalia (1936–2022)

Everest summiteer and champion of people with disabilities

Hari Pal Singh Ahluwalia was born on 6 November 1936 and grew up in Shimla, where his father was a civil engineer in the Central Public Works Department. He studied at St George’s College, Mussoorie, where he took up rock climbing and photography, and trained at the Himalayan Mountaineering Institute, Darjeeling. On 29 May 1965, exactly twelve years after the first ascent of Everest, he reached the summit with H. C. S. Rawat and Phu Dorjee Sherpa as part of Captain Kohli’s expedition.

A few months later, in the 1965 India–Pakistan war, a bullet injury to his spine left him in a wheelchair. He went on to found the Indian Spinal Injuries Centre in New Delhi in 1993 and wrote several books, including Higher than Everest. He received the Arjuna Award, the Padma Shri and the Padma Bhushan, and died in New Delhi on 14 January 2022.

Ravindar Singh Somi (b. 1944)

Scout and Himalayan enthusiast

Ravindar Singh Somi was born on 25 September 1944 at Landour, Mussoorie, and rose through the Scout movement from Cub Scout to its highest levels of training. He ran open Cub, Scout and Rover groups in the Doon Valley, became Assistant District Scout Commissioner for Dehradun, earned the Scout and Cub Wood Badges under the Gilwell Park training scheme, and was Deputy Camp Chief and Chief Public Relations Officer of the All India Boy Scouts Association, receiving its Long Service Medal, Medal of Merit and Silver Star.

A founder member of the Climbers & Explorers Club of India, he edited its journal Abhiyatri and later the book The Incredible Himalayas. As a director of the Himalayan Environment Trust he worked closely with Sir Edmund Hillary and edited its e-journal Himalayan Online.
